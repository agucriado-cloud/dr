Step 6, made concreteThe Stress-Test Lab

Marks's stress test asks one question above all: in the bad case, is this a recoverable paper loss, or a permanent one? A drawdown you can hold through is volatility; a drawdown that forces you to sell is the permanent loss that actually matters. These three tools put that question on the table.

Keep the boundary in mind. These are judgment aids, not forecasts. Marks distrusts models fed by historical inputs — "garbage in, garbage out," with the added danger that the math lends false comfort. The numbers below illustrate the shape of outcomes and the survival question; they don't predict, and they never override your judgment. Change the assumptions and watch what moves.

1 · Downside Survival Test

Does the bad case stay a paper loss, or does it force selling? Set your mix and a shock, and watch whether your cash and reserves can carry your spending without liquidating depressed assets.

4 · Concentration vs. Over-Diversification

Marks's "dare to be great" tension. You can't beat the index if you've quietly rebuilt it. Each name beyond your best ideas dilutes your conviction and drags the portfolio toward the average. Drag the count and watch your best ideas get watered down.

The boundary, honestly. This illustrates Marks's judgment — diversifying into too many names dilutes your edge and recreates the benchmark (Dare to Be Great, 2006/2014). The precise mathematics of how many names cut risk, and how correlation changes the answer, is the mechanics layer Marks doesn't teach — don't read exact risk-reduction figures into this picture.

Drill · ongoingThe Decision Journal
